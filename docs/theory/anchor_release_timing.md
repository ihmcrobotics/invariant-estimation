# Anchor Release Timing, and the Vertical Sink

The estimator sinks: the base-height estimate drifts downward at a roughly constant rate for as
long as the robot walks. This note derives why, shows that the cause is **when** a contact anchor
is released rather than **how tightly** it is held, and states the property a fix must satisfy.

Every number quoted here is measured, at $N=4$ (toe/heel) on `data/dr5`, by
`experiments/process_socket_ablation.py`. See [](#the-arms) for what each arm changes.

## The sink is an integrated velocity bias, not a position offset

Measured closed loop over 30 s, comparing the mean vertical velocity error against the least-squares
slope of the height error:

| filter | $\overline{e_{vz}}$ [m/s] | $\mathrm{d}(\Delta z)/\mathrm{d}t$ [m/s] |
|---|---|---|
| $N{=}4$ analytic, no network | $-0.0134$ | $-0.0154$ |
| $N{=}4$ + ContactNet run 6 | $-0.0039$ | $-0.0037$ |
| $N{=}4$ + ContactNet run 7 | $-0.0347$ | $-0.0375$ |

The two agree in every arm, so the sink *is* $\int e_{vz}\,\mathrm{d}t$. It is present with **no
network in the loop at all**, so it is generated by the analytic filter; a learned $\Sigma_C$ only
modulates it, by roughly $\pm 3\times$ in either direction.

## Why the contact update can push the base down

The contact measurement is the body-frame FK vector $y_i = \hat R^\top(d_i - p)$, with world
residual and Jacobian

$$
\nu_i = \hat R y_i - (\hat d_i - \hat p),
\qquad
H_i = \begin{bmatrix} 0_{3\times3} & 0_{3\times3} & +I_3 & \cdots & -I_3 & \cdots \end{bmatrix}
$$ (eq:contact-h)

in the tangent ordering (rotation $0$, base velocity $3$, base position $6$, contact $i$ at
$9+3i$; invariant **I4**). The $-I_3$ sits on contact $i$'s own block.

**$H_i$ is a difference operator.** It observes only $d_i - p$, so the update is structurally
incapable of distinguishing "the base moved down" from "the anchor moved up". Which of the two
absorbs the residual is decided entirely by the prior. Writing $P_{pp}$, $P_{pd}$, $P_{dd}$ for the
base-position, cross, and contact blocks,

$$
S = H_i P H_i^\top + \bar N
= \underbrace{P_{pp} - P_{pd} - P_{dp} + P_{dd}}_{\textstyle \Sigma_{\mathrm{rel}}} + \bar N ,
\qquad
K = P H_i^\top S^{-1}
$$ (eq:innovation-cov)

and the fraction of the residual delivered to the **base position** is

$$
f \;=\; \frac{P_{pp} - P_{pd}}{\Sigma_{\mathrm{rel}} + \bar N} .
$$ (eq:apportionment)

Only the **relative** covariance $\Sigma_{\mathrm{rel}}$ appears. That is the whole point: global
position is unobservable, so $P_{pp}$ and $P_{dd}$ are both dominated by a large common mode which
cancels in the difference. Reading absolute variances tells you nothing about apportionment.

### What the filter actually does, measured

Per contact, at three gait phases:

| phase | $P_{pp}$ | $P_{pd}$ | $P_{dd}$ | $\Sigma_{\mathrm{rel}}$ | $f$ | velocity gain | $\nu_z$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| late stance | 0.2106 | 0.2106 | 0.2108 | $2\times10^{-4}$ | **0.538** | 2.425 | $+5.4\times10^{-5}$ |
| early swing | 0.2106 | 0.2106 | 0.3106 | $1\times10^{-1}$ | $-4.3\times10^{-5}$ | $9.4\times10^{-4}$ | $+2.7\times10^{-4}$ |
| late swing | 0.2107 | 0.2106 | 0.3106 | $1\times10^{-1}$ | — | $2.6\times10^{-6}$ | $-2.0\times10^{-4}$ |

The absolute variances are ~0.21 in every cell and irrelevant. $\Sigma_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is
**500× tighter in late stance than in swing**, and $f$ correspondingly goes from $0.54$ to
$\sim 0$.

The stance tightness is *correct*, not a defect. A full stance of contact updates drives
$P_{pd}\to P_{pp}$ and $P_{dd}\to P_{pp}$, because throughout that stance the foot really was
world-static and FK really was consistent with it. The filter has learned, correctly, that it knows
where the foot is relative to the base.

## The bias is a half-wave rectification

At end of stance the foot begins to unload and roll before it leaves the ground. FK reports it
rising relative to the base while $\hat d_i$ is still pinned where stance left it, so
$\nu_z > 0$. With the update convention $\hat X^+ = \exp\!\big(-(K\nu)^\wedge\big)\hat X$
(invariant **I5**) and $f \approx 0.54$, about half of that residual is spent pushing the base
**down**.

Feet rise at liftoff and never fall, so the sign is the same at every liftoff on every foot. The
error therefore *accumulates* instead of averaging out — a bias, not noise.

The measured $\nu_z$ column above shows the rectification directly. The residual **does** reverse
sign, going negative in late swing. But the velocity gain there is $2.6\times10^{-6}$ against
$2.425$ in late stance — a ratio of $2584$ measured over 494 liftoffs. **A signed residual is
being half-wave rectified by a phase-dependent gain**: the positive half is applied with a gain
$\sim10^{6}$ larger than the negative half.

## Why $\Sigma_C$ cannot fix it, and a timer can

$\Sigma_C$ is the process noise on the anchor; it reaches the contact block of $Q_d$ through
`contact.digest` and inflates $P_{dd}$ each tick. It is the physically correct lever — what breaks
at liftoff is the assumption that $d_i$ is world-static, and that assumption lives in the
**process** model, not the measurement (see the DECISION note in `inEKF/filter.py`: masking the
measurement would be wrong, because the encoders still locate the foot perfectly well during
swing).

But raising the **stance level** of $\Sigma_C$ does nothing, because stance $\Sigma_{\mathrm{rel}}$
is a *fixed point*: process noise pushes it up each tick, and every contact update pulls it back
down. The equilibrium depends on the ratio of the two, and — decisively — the level does not move
the **phase** at which the loose regime begins. Measured (arm C, stance chol swept two decades):

| stance chol | $10^{-4}$ | $10^{-3}$ | $10^{-2}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| slope $e_{pz}$ [m/s] | $-0.01486$ | $-0.01486$ | $-0.01469$ |

Flat, to 1%.

Moving the transition **earlier** works, because it makes $\Sigma_{\mathrm{rel}}$ already large when
the liftoff residual appears, so $f\to 0$ and the residual is absorbed by the anchor — which is
where it belongs. Measured (arm B, the loose state dilated $n$ ticks backwards from liftoff):

| lead [ticks] | 0 | 10 | 25 | 50 | 100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| slope $e_{pz}$ [m/s] | $-0.01486$ | $-0.01232$ | $-0.00940$ | $-0.00609$ | $\mathbf{-0.00344}$ |
| height rms [m] | 0.1563 | 0.1296 | 0.1007 | 0.0724 | **0.0551** |

**$4.3\times$ on the sink and $2.8\times$ on height rms, from a process-side change alone.** Tilt
improves too ($0.510^\circ \to 0.465^\circ$), so this is not a trade.

### The transition cannot simply be deleted

Holding $\Sigma_C$ constant at every phase — no stance/swing switching at all — is catastrophic
(arm I): velocity rms $1.15$ against $0.059$, tilt $10.4^\circ$ against $0.51^\circ$. The tight
stance regime is precisely what makes base **velocity** observable; a world-static anchor is the
only zero-velocity information the filter has. The transition must be *placed* correctly, not
removed.

## Why $N{=}4$ recovers less than $N{=}2$ did

The same sweep at $N=2$ on `data/dr` gave $19\times$; at $N=4$ it gives $4.3\times$. Toe/heel puts
two anchors per foot, at the fore and aft edges of the same sole plane, and at liftoff the heel
unloads before the toe. The two contacts' doses are therefore staggered rather than simultaneous,
and partially self-cancelling — part of the fix is already banked in the geometry. This is
consistent with the $N=4$ analytic filter sinking less than $N=2$ did before any intervention.

## What a fix must do

The world-static assumption stops holding when the foot **begins to unload and roll**. Every
contact detector in the port fires when load **crosses a threshold**, which is necessarily later:
`ContactTrust` is a Schmitt trigger on normalised load, and even with immediate release it can only
fire once the load has already gone. **A threshold on load *level*, however low, is structurally
late. An anticipatory signal must depend on the load's *rate* or on its ratio to the stance peak.**

### The property test

Do not test a release schedule by its sink number alone — that is an integrated, end-to-end
quantity that many wrong things can improve. Test the mechanism:

> **$f$ must have collapsed before $\nu_z$ turns positive.**

Concretely, at every liftoff, the apportionment fraction {eq}`eq:apportionment` should already be
at its swing value ($\lesssim 10^{-3}$) by the first tick at which $\nu_z > 0$. That is a
per-liftoff assertion over the ~494 liftoffs in a replay, it is causal, and it fails loudly for a
schedule that merely loosens *more* rather than *earlier*.

## The arms

`experiments/process_socket_ablation.py`. Every arm replays the same recorded rollout from the same
truth seed and differs in exactly one field of `InEKFInputs`, so any difference is attributable.
The heuristic $\Sigma_C$ is a two-level square wave: stance ⇒ chol $10^{-4}$, swing ⇒ chol $10^{1}$,
switched by contact trust.

| arm | what it changes | question |
|---|---|---|
| A | nothing — the recorded heuristic | baseline |
| B | dilates the swing (loose) value $n$ ticks **backwards** | is it timing? |
| C | same switching, different **stance level** | is it tightness? |
| E | **causal** early release from recorded normal force | can a real predictor do it? |
| I | a constant chol at every phase | what does training start from? |
| F | sweeps `contact_floor` | what is the floor buying? |
| D / N | a network on the measurement / process socket | control, head-to-head |

Arm B is a running **maximum** over $[t, t+n]$, not a shift — a shift would move touchdown earlier
too, tightening the anchor before the foot is down, which is a second change in the opposite
direction. It is **non-causal by construction** and is a mechanism test, never a deployable filter.
Arm E is the causal counterpart, and arm B is its ceiling.
